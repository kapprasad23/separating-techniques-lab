Stage 1: Heating the Saltwater Solution

The saltwater solution (soluble salt dissolved in liquid water) is placed in an open evaporating basin resting on wire gauze over a Bunsen burner.

🔬 Particle-Level Movement

Water molecules (H₂O) absorb thermal energy and move faster, while dissolved sodium (Na⁺) and chloride (Cl⁻) ions remain dispersed throughout the liquid.

Apparatus & Design Inspector Click any part below
Evaporating Basin Wide Surface Area

A shallow, wide open porcelain dish that exposes a large surface area of the solution to the air, allowing water vapour to escape rapidly during heating.

🌊 Real-World Connection

Harvesting Sea Salt by Solar Evaporation

Sea water is trapped in shallow coastal salt pans. Heat from the sun and wind naturally evaporate the water over days, leaving behind mounds of sea salt ready for collection and purification.

Think Critically • Pages 120 & 121

Critical Thinking Scenarios

Scenario 1 (Page 120): Kerosene + Petrol Mixture

"Which separation technique will you suggest to separate a mixture containing kerosene and petrol and why?"

Distillation (specifically Fractional Distillation): Kerosene and petrol are both miscible liquids obtained from crude oil, but they have different boiling points (petrol boils at a lower temperature range, roughly 40–120 °C, than kerosene at 150–300 °C). When heated, petrol vaporises first, condenses in the condenser, and is collected separately!
Scenario 2 (Page 121): Stranded on a Deserted Island

"How would you obtain drinking water if you are stranded on a deserted island which has not been having rainfall for some time?"

Solar / Improvised Distillation of Seawater: You cannot drink seawater directly because of its high salt content. By heating seawater (over a fire or in a solar still pit covered with a sheet angled toward a cup), water evaporates leaving the salt behind, and the water vapour condenses on the cooler cover surface to drip down as pure drinkable water (distillate)!

Section 11.2 • Page 121

Why Purification Matters in Medicine, Food & Industry

💎 Crystallisation in Daily Life

Used in purifying seawater, sugars, and fats. It is also used to control crystal size and improve the smooth texture of ice cream, fudge, and chocolate, as well as making medicinal rock candies.

💧 Distillation in Industry

Essential for commercial refining of crude oil into gasoline (petrol), paraffin, and kerosene, and for producing distilled water for laboratories, car batteries, and sterile medical injections.

☁️ Sublimation in Products

Solid air fresheners (including blocks hanging in toilets) and mothballs (naphthalene/camphor) sublime directly into fragrant gas. Also widely used in the dye-sublimation printing industry!

Section 11.3 • Question 12 (Page 122)

Contributions of Classical Muslim Chemists to Separation & Purification

Page 122 highlights the pioneering work of Jabir ibn Hayyan (Geber), Al-Kindi (Alkindus), and Al-Razi (Rhazes) in developing modern laboratory separation techniques:

Jabir ibn Hayyan (Geber) c. 721–815 CE

Invented the alembic (al-anbiq) still for efficient distillation. He systematically documented crystallisation (taballur), sublimation (tas'id), evaporation (tabkhir), and filtration to purify acids, salts, and metals.

Al-Kindi (Alkindus) c. 801–873 CE

Authored the Book of the Chemistry of Perfume and Distillations containing over 100 recipes for extracting pure essential oils, rosewater, and camphor using controlled hydro-distillation and sublimation.

Al-Razi (Rasis / Rhazes) c. 854–925 CE

In Kitab al-Asrar (Book of Secrets), he classified laboratory apparatus (alembics, cucurbits, water baths, funnels, filters) and used distillation to purify alcohol (ethanol) and kerosene as well as sublimation for medicinal compounds.
